## 1\. Executive Summary

Recykal should be known to approximately **15 stakeholder clusters** spanning Indian regulators, urban local bodies, EPR-obligated brand owners, formal and informal value-chain actors, climate/impact investors, technology partners, global multilateral plastics bodies, standards and ratings houses, industry associations, media, awards platforms, academia, philanthropy/DFIs, and peer companies.

**The three highest-leverage Tier 1 audiences:**

1. **EPR-obligated brand owners and their sustainability leaders** — Recykal's paying customers  
2. **Climate / circular-economy investors** — for the next priced round and international expansion financing  
3. **Indian policy and regulatory bodies** (MoEFCC, CPCB, priority SPCBs) — they write the EPR rules that create Recykal's demand

**Why the timing is favorable:**

- India's Plastic Waste Management (Amendment) Rules tightened EPR traceability: from **1 July 2025**, plastic packaging must display the PIBO's registered name, CPCB EPR registration number, and plastic category via text, QR code or barcode — with recycled-content and food-contact declarations added from **FY2025-26**. \[A\]  
- New mandates require rigid plastic packaging to contain a minimum of **30% post-consumer recycled (PCR) content** in the FY2025–26 cycle, with non-compliance exposing brands to environmental compensation fines and suspension of operating licenses. \[B\]  
- The **UN Global Plastics Treaty stalled** at INC-5.2 (Geneva, August 2025\) and reconvened only administratively at **INC-5.3 (Geneva, 7 February 2026\)** — making credible, data-driven private-sector infrastructure central to the "we won't recycle our way out, but we need systems" narrative. \[A\]  
- A severe **trust deficit in EPR credits** creates direct demand for traceability (see §4.2).

**Strategic framing:** Traditional waste frameworks collapse through **"silo failure"** — systemic fragmentation in which waste generators, informal collectors, aggregators, processors, recyclers, brand owners and state authorities operate without data-driven coordination. Recykal exists to unify these actors through cloud computing and machine learning delivering unit-level traceability. Its stated strategic vision: drive behavioral change for **1 billion people** and divert at least **10% of India's waste** back into the circular economy. \[B\]\[C\]

## 4\. Market & Regulatory Context

### 4.1 India — the core demand driver

Every tightening of India's EPR regime expands Recykal's addressable market. Awareness among regulators and obligated brands is therefore simultaneously a policy-influence and a sales imperative. \[A\]

- **\~18,000 companies** using plastic packaging registered with CPCB under the EPR scheme (2022-23, Ministry data via Climate Samurai), with a collective recycling target of **\~3.3 million tonnes** \[A\]  
- **Plastic Waste Management (Amendment) Rules**: labelling/traceability mandates live from 1 July 2025; recycled-content and food-contact declarations from FY2025-26 \[A\]  
- **30% minimum PCR content** for rigid plastic packaging; penalties include environmental compensation fines and suspension of operating licenses \[B\]  
- **E-Waste Management Rules** govern electronics OEMs \[C\]  
- **End-of-Life Vehicle (ELV)**, industrial scrap, rubber, and lead-acid/lithium-ion battery streams are expanding regulated categories \[C\]  
- **Swachh Bharat Mission-Urban 2.0 (SBM-U 2.0)** drives ULB landfill-diversion obligations \[A\]\[C\]

### 4.2 The EPR credit trust deficit — Recykal's sharpest wedge

CPCB and state boards found **700,000 fake EPR certificates** — *"38 times more than the certificate generation capacity of recyclers"* — and imposed a cumulative **₹355 crore fine**, per a Centre for Science and Environment (CSE) analysis reported by Down To Earth (July 2024). CSE's **Atin Biswas** noted this implies CPCB's **4.1 MT** annual plastic-waste estimate *"is an underestimation."* Recykal's traceability is a direct answer to this. \[A\]

### Cluster 2 — Urban Local Bodies, Smart Cities & International Public Agencies (Tier 1 — B2G revenue)

**Why they matter:** ULBs are shifting from legacy landfill-centric models to data-rich circularity, and are the buyers of Smart Centre and dDRS deployments. Most struggle with inefficient manual collection networks, opaque operations at dry resource collection centres (DRCCs), and overfilled landfills. \[B\]\[C\]

**Indian ULBs:** Municipal corporations across Tier-1 metros (**Bengaluru, Mumbai, Hyderabad**) plus emerging Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities, all legally obligated under SBM-U 2.0. **Recykal partners with 100+ ULBs**, enabling automated record-keeping, inflow/outflow tracking, logistics optimization and **50,000+ MT/month landfill diversion**. Operational flow: unsegregated dry waste → SmartSkan (Google CircularNet) sorting at \>90% precision → high-purity feedstock routed to re-processors at a 6x value multiplier → real-time data feeds into municipal and state regulatory dashboards. \[C\]

**Flagship public-sector case study — Uttarakhand (Kedarnath) Digital DRS** ✅ Serialized QR codes plus UPI-linked refunds recovered **1.63 lakh bottles**, preventing plastic from entering Himalayan rivers. Run on the Char Dham route with the **Rudraprayag District Administration**; won the **Digital India Award 2022 (Silver)**. Demonstrates the "so what" layer: digital incentives drive mass behavioral change more effectively than traditional enforcement. Additional dDRS deployment at **Odisha's Bali Yatra**. \[A\]\[B\]

**International municipalities & Global South public agencies:** India's structural conditions — high informal participation, limited sorting infrastructure, budget constraints — mirror South Asia, Southeast Asia, Africa and Latin America. **Gelephu Smart City administration (Bhutan)** has deployed Recykal's dDRS. The software-first architecture minimizes dependence on expensive conventional RVMs. \[C\]

**Also relevant:** state tourism departments (Retearn RVMs / digital DRS for incentivized returns at pilgrimage and tourist sites)
